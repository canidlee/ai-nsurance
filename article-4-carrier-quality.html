How to judge whether your insurance carrier is actually good (before you need to file a claim)

Every carrier sounds great in their ads. The ones with the most reassuring marketing are not always the ones who pay claims fastest. Here's how to run the check now.

The problem with insurance is that you only discover whether your carrier is good at the worst possible moment — when you're standing in front of a flooded basement, a totaled car, or a fire-damaged home, calling a 1-800 number and hoping for the best.

Every carrier sounds great in their ads. The ones with the most reassuring marketing are not always the ones who pay claims fastest, dispute them least, or stay stable in their pricing over time. Here is how to evaluate your carrier now, while it's a low-stakes research exercise.

The four things that actually matter

1. Claims satisfaction (most important)

Insurance is a promise: pay premiums now, get money when something goes wrong. Claims satisfaction measures how well carriers keep that promise — speed of response, fairness of settlement, ease of the process, and whether you had to fight for what you were owed.

J.D. Power publishes two annual studies: the Auto Claims Satisfaction Study and the Home Insurance Study. They survey tens of thousands of actual claimants — people who filed a real claim. Higher is better; anything below the industry average is a meaningful warning sign.

What it predicts: A carrier ranked in the bottom quartile for claims satisfaction doesn't suddenly improve for your claim. These scores are sticky. A carrier that routinely generates complaints about delayed adjusters and lowballed settlements will likely bring that behavior to your claim too.

2. Complaint ratio (most objective)

Every state tracks formal consumer complaints. The NAIC compiles this into a complaint ratio — complaints per unit of market share — and publishes it free at naic.org.

  • 1.0 = industry average number of complaints
  • 0.5 = half as many as average — a good sign
  • 2.0 = twice as many — a significant warning

This is the most objective data point available because it's based on formal regulatory complaints — cases serious enough to escalate to a regulator, not just survey responses.

How to check: Go to naic.org/consumer/ → Company Search → look up your carrier. The complaint index is listed separately for auto and homeowners.

3. Financial strength (solvency risk)

This matters most in catastrophic regional events — hurricane, wildfire, widespread flooding — when a carrier faces a surge of claims and must have the reserves to pay them all.

AM Best is the primary rating agency for insurance companies. Their ratings run from A++ (superior) down through A, A-, B+, B, and so on. Any carrier rated below B+ should be scrutinized. A- or above is comfortable.

Where to check: ambest.com — look up any licensed carrier's rating for free.

4. Price stability over time

This is the dimension almost no comparison site measures — because measuring it requires multi-year data, and comparison sites make money on transactions.

A carrier's ability to offer a competitive year-one rate tells you almost nothing about what they'll charge in year three. Some carriers deliberately price new-customer policies below their long-term actuarial target and make up the difference through above-average renewal increases in years two through four.

How to assess it:

  • Pull your last three renewal notices. Has the premium increased faster than general inflation?
  • Search "[carrier name] rate increase complaints" on Reddit's r/personalfinance. Patterns show up quickly.
  • Check your state DOI rate filing history — carriers file proposed rate changes with regulators and these are public.

The carriers that consistently score well

USAA is in a category of its own for military families who qualify. Their JD Power scores, complaint ratios, and retention rates are consistently the best in the industry — sometimes by a significant margin. If you or a family member has served in the military, determine whether you qualify.

Amica Mutual is consistently second in most national quality assessments. They are a mutual company — owned by policyholders, not shareholders — which structurally aligns their incentives differently than publicly traded carriers.

Erie Insurance consistently scores above average in claims satisfaction with a very low complaint ratio. Available in 12 states.

Auto-Owners Insurance scores consistently well. Available through independent agents in 26 states.

The common thread: these are not the carriers running the most television advertising. Marketing budgets and claims-paying quality are often inversely correlated in this industry.

Patterns worth investigating

High complaint ratios: Any carrier with a complaint ratio consistently above 1.2–1.5 is generating more disputes than their peers. Check the trend over three years, not just the current year.

Declining JD Power scores: A single below-average year might be noise. A carrier that has dropped two or more tiers over three consecutive annual studies is showing a structural problem.

Aggressive entry pricing: If a carrier quoted you 30% below your current carrier with identical coverage, that's either an error or a deliberate underpricing strategy. Neither is good for year three.

Carriers withdrawing from states: Several major carriers have announced partial or full withdrawal from California, Florida, and Louisiana. A carrier actively reducing exposure in your state is worth monitoring.

How to run this check in 20 minutes

  1. Go to naic.org and look up your carrier's complaint ratio for your coverage type
  2. Search "[your carrier] JD Power 2024 ranking" for their most recent score
  3. Search "[your carrier] AM Best rating" to verify financial strength
  4. Pull your last three renewal notices and calculate annual premium change percentage

If anything raises a concern, the next step isn't necessarily to switch — it's to understand what you'd be switching to. A slightly imperfect carrier you know is often better than an unknown one you don't.
