How to read your insurance declarations page (and the 6 numbers that actually matter)

Your dec page is a one-page summary of every coverage decision your insurer made on your behalf. Most people file it without reading it. Here's what to actually look at.

Your insurance declarations page — the "dec page" — is the one-page summary sent at the start of every policy term. Most people file it without reading it. That's a mistake, because every coverage decision your insurer made on your behalf is summarized right there: what's covered, what isn't, and how much you'd actually receive if something went wrong.

This guide walks you through every section, explains what the numbers mean, and flags the six figures worth scrutinizing every single renewal.

What a declarations page actually is

It's not your full policy. The dec page is a summary — think of it as the receipt for the insurance you bought. The full policy document (which can run 40–60 pages) is the legal contract. The dec page is the human-readable version of the key terms.

It arrives at the start of every new policy period, typically with renewal paperwork. If you switched carriers, you'll receive one from both the old and new insurer. It's also what a lender or landlord asks for when they need proof of insurance.

The six numbers that actually matter

1. Coverage limits

For auto insurance, you'll see something like 100/300/100 or written out as three separate numbers. These are your liability limits:

  • The first number ($100,000) is the maximum your insurer pays for bodily injury to one person in an accident you caused
  • The second number ($300,000) is the maximum for all people injured in a single accident
  • The third number ($100,000) is your property damage liability limit

State minimums are dramatically lower — often 25/50/25 or even 15/30/15. If you're carrying state minimums, you're underinsured the moment you cause an accident with more than one injured person in the other car.

What to look for: If your limits are at or near your state minimum, you're exposed. The gap between a $25,000 limit and a $150,000 hospital bill is yours to cover personally.

2. Comprehensive vs. collision deductibles

These are listed separately and they mean different things:

  • Collision covers damage when your car hits something — another car, a guardrail, a tree
  • Comprehensive covers everything else — theft, hail, a deer, a falling branch, flood

Your deductible is what you pay before insurance kicks in. A $1,000 deductible means you absorb the first $1,000 of any claim. Higher deductibles mean lower premiums, but the math only works if you have that cash available.

What to look for: If your car is worth less than $4,000–5,000, carrying comprehensive and collision may not be worth the premium. Use the Kelly Blue Book value of your car and compare it to your annual premium plus your deductible.

3. Uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage (UM/UIM)

This is the coverage most people skip or minimize — and it's one of the most important on the page.

About 13% of drivers nationwide are uninsured. In some states it's over 20%. If an uninsured driver hits you and causes serious injury, your own UM coverage is what pays your medical bills and lost wages. Without it, you may be suing an uninsured driver in court — which is slow, expensive, and often uncollectable.

What to look for: UM/UIM limits should match your liability limits. If you're carrying 100/300 liability but only 25/50 UM, the mismatch leaves you exposed to exactly the scenario this coverage is designed for.

4. ACV vs. replacement cost (home insurance)

This is the single most important distinction on a homeowners dec page, and the one most people don't notice until they file a claim.

  • Actual cash value (ACV): Your insurer pays what your damaged property is worth today — depreciated. A five-year-old roof that cost $18,000 to install might be valued at $9,000 after depreciation. You get $9,000 and absorb the rest.
  • Replacement cost value (RCV): Your insurer pays what it costs to actually fix or replace the item with new materials. Same roof — you get the $22,000 it costs to replace it today.

The premium difference between ACV and RCV is usually small. The claims difference can be enormous.

What to look for: Find the words "actual cash value" or "replacement cost" on your dec page under dwelling coverage and personal property. If it says ACV and you haven't chosen this consciously, call your agent.

5. Dwelling coverage amount

This is the dollar amount your insurer will pay to rebuild your home if it's destroyed. It's not your home's market value or what you paid for it — it's the estimated cost to reconstruct the structure from scratch.

These two numbers diverge significantly in many markets. In California, Texas, and Florida especially, construction costs have risen 30–50% since 2020. Policies that haven't been updated are now dramatically underinsured.

What to look for: Ask your insurer for the "replacement cost estimator" calculation they used. If your dwelling coverage is $350,000 but local construction costs suggest your home would cost $520,000 to rebuild, you have a $170,000 gap that you'd absorb personally.

6. Policy effective dates and named insured

Simple, but catches people off guard. Verify:

  • The policy start and end dates are correct
  • Your name (and a spouse's, if applicable) is listed as named insured
  • The vehicle VIN or property address is correct

A VIN mismatch — especially common when you lease or buy a new car — means a claim could technically be denied. It happens.

What's usually not on the dec page (but should be)

A few things your dec page won't tell you that you should ask about separately:

Your complaint ratio. Your carrier's NAIC complaint index is public information. A ratio above 1.0 means more complaints than average for their market share. Check our carrier quality guide →

Rate trajectory. The dec page shows this year's premium, not how it's changed over three years. Pull your last three renewal notices and compare.

Applied discounts. Some carriers list discounts applied; others don't. Call and ask for a "discount review" — a specific request that signals you know what you're talking about.

How to use this at renewal

Every time your renewal arrives, before you pay, do a four-minute check:

  1. Have your liability limits changed?
  2. Is your dwelling coverage keeping pace with local construction costs?
  3. Is your deductible still appropriate for your financial situation?
  4. Are UM/UIM limits matching your liability limits?
  5. Is the coverage still ACV or RCV — did anything quietly change?

The insurer won't call you when something changes. That's what this is for.
